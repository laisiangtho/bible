### The Endless Negativity of Expressive Adjectives in Zolai

Zolai contains an endless supply of negative adjectives that are rarely found in other languages or formal writing. I believe we could actually use programming to map or solve these terms.

Structurally, these words always consist of two rhyming or repeating syllables, where the second syllable is a slight variation of the first.

Examples: lenglang, pelpal, pilpul, kehkuh, kengkang

When spoken, these words are often paired with particles like "cin," "cia," or "laizang." They are mainly used to criticize someone's appearance or status.

Interestingly, changing the vowels alters the emotional tone. While most variations remain critical, modifying the vowel sound can soften the meaning—making it sound affectionate, like talking to or about a toddler:

Slightly softened/playful: lengleng, pelpel, pilpil, kehkeh, kengkeng

- Heavy/intense: langlang, palpal, pulpul, kuhkuh, kangkang
- A great example of this distinction comes from Dr. Enno:

"Kiit lunglung kei nila, kiit lengleng zawni."

Roughly translated, this means: "Let's not love each other in a cold, chaotic way—let's love each other warmly and tenderly." While some might think lunglung and lengleng describe the group size (like a crowd vs. a family), they actually describe the attitude or quality of the love itself: lunglung carries a heavy, messy, or negative vibe, whereas lengleng expresses a sweet, genuine closeness.

What makes these words unique is that their exact definitions are almost impossible to translate. If a non-native speaker asks what one means, a native speaker usually can't give a dictionary definition—they can only point to a visual example or demonstrate the condition!
